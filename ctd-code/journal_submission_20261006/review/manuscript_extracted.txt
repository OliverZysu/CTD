Abstract
Background: Chemical–disease databases combine environmental exposures, therapeutic effects and
inferred associations. Graph learning can prioritize relationships for review, but prediction scores,
retrievable publications and shared genes represent different forms of evidence. We examine how these
layers can support an evidence-linked chemical–disease workflow.
Methods: We combined a retrospective assessment of existing graph-learning experiments with
descriptive analysis of retained prediction records, aggregate literature-retrieval results and a shared-
gene screen. The study inventory reports 9,546,982 Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD)
relationship records and 3,125 manually extracted exposure records. The retained code defines a
two-layer GraphSAGE baseline for the chemical–disease modeling component; literature and gene
overlap provide separate evidence views.
Results: In the reported exposure-data pilot, a multilayer perceptron achieved an area under the receiver
operating characteristic curve of 0.9392, compared with 0.9382 for a graph attention network and 0.9254
for a graph convolutional network. Reported literature-retrieval yield was 30.4% in the highest-ranked
1,000-record sample and 7.2% in 1,000 random pairs, a descriptive ratio of 4.22. The retained ranking
contains 62,042 misclassified records and lacks class labels; its scores cannot be interpreted uniformly
as association probabilities. A separate shared-gene screen reported 128 supported records among
639 mappable records in a 1,000-record sample. The overall rate, 12.8%, was below the recorded
disease-label permutation mean of 18.7%.
Conclusions: The results motivate literature-guided review of chemical–disease predictions but do not
establish prospective discovery, causal effects or superiority of graph learning. An extensible evidence
interface should preserve relation type, denominator, source publication and gene-path provenance.
Broader chemical coverage and closer Acemap integration are specified as extensions, with validation
requiring reproducible predictions and independent evidence.
Keywords: chemical–disease associations; comparative toxicogenomics; graph neural networks; literature
evidence; environmental exposure; knowledge graphs

Background
Environmental and pharmacological chemicals interact with biological systems across a wide range of
exposure settings. Their recorded relationships with disease can describe adverse effects, treatment,
molecular perturbation or statistical association. These meanings matter when a database is used to generate
hypotheses: a chemical that treats a condition and a chemical associated with increased risk should not
become indistinguishable evidence merely because both connect to the same disease node.
The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) organizes literature-curated chemical, gene and
disease knowledge and derives additional relationships through shared biological information [1]. It offers
an appropriate foundation for broad chemical–disease analysis, while manually extracted environmental
studies can add exposure concentration, population and effect-estimate context. These resources differ in
their units of evidence. A curated record, an inferred relationship and an epidemiological observation are
not interchangeable measurements, and several records may refer to the same chemical–disease pair.

Graph learning provides a way to use the organization of observed relationships when scoring candidate
pairs. Graph convolutional networks (GCNs), graph attention networks (GATs) and GraphSAGE aggregate
information over neighborhoods using different operators [2–4]. In drug–disease prediction, layer-attention
GCNs incorporate similarity networks and combine information across layers, while multi-relational
approaches integrate additional biological relations [5, 6]. These methods motivate graph-based exploration,
but their published performance does not establish performance on a different chemical inventory or
evaluation split.
For a scientific discovery interface, a score becomes more useful when the user can examine the evidence
behind a candidate. Three questions are distinct: whether a model ranks a recorded relationship highly,
whether a publication discusses the pair, and whether a biological path provides relevant mechanistic
context. Literature retrieval can recover a treatment report or an indirect adverse-event pathway; shared
genes can arise because certain genes and diseases are extensively studied. Neither signal alone establishes
a new causal relationship.
This study organizes an existing chemical–disease project around these distinctions. We describe the
implemented association-modeling baseline, examine the available pilot and evidence-review results, and
formulate an evidence-linked interface for broader chemical coverage. The contribution is an exploratory
workflow and a quantitative account of its evidence limits, rather than a new graph architecture. We ask
whether the reported rankings yield useful literature leads, whether simple gene overlap strengthens their
interpretation, and what information must be preserved before candidate relationships can be presented as
discoveries.

Methods
2.1
Study design and evidence sources
The analysis has three components: a pilot comparison on literature-extracted exposure data, retrospective
examination of CTD-based prediction records, and separate literature and gene-context screens. The
database inventory and original pilot metrics are study-level summaries. We additionally counted and
characterized the retained prediction CSV directly. Source-level provenance and the distinction between
reported and recomputed quantities are provided in Supplementary Information, Tables S1–S4.
The study inventory comprises 9,546,982 CTD chemical–disease relationship records and 3,125 envi-
ronmental exposure records extracted manually from epidemiological literature (Table 1). The inventory
reports 108,217 CTD records with direct evidence. These are historical project counts; they do not describe
the size of the current CTD release. A relationship record may encode an evidence type or an inference
context, so record counts must not be read as counts of unique graph edges. The original CTD snapshot
and the row-level environmental dataset are not available for this retrospective analysis, which prevents
independent confirmation of graph deduplication and source dates.

2.2
Chemical–disease graph and task definition
Let C and D denote the chemical and disease node sets. The binary baseline associates each observed pair
(c,d) with y = 1 and samples unobserved pairs with y = 0. Here, “unobserved” means absent from the
selected input records, not experimentally shown to have no relationship. This distinction is important
because database coverage is incomplete and because direct and inferred evidence can coexist.
The following graph construction and model description come from static inspection of retained source
code. They are not a recovered manifest of the historical pilot or screening runs. The retained loader
samples one negative record per positive record by independently selecting chemical and disease nodes
and rejecting known positive pairs. Accepted negatives can repeat. It shuffles records and splits each
label class into approximately 80% training, 10% validation and 10% test data using a NumPy seed of 42.
Message-passing edges contain training positives only and are shared by the three splits. In the retained
implementation, edges are directed from chemical to disease; reverse edges are not appended.
The split operates on records rather than grouped unique pairs. The CSV cleaning step removes duplicate
triples of chemical, disease and evidence type before binary label collapse. Multiple evidence types may
consequently leave repeated positive pairs; repeated negatives can also cross split boundaries. These
properties create a possible overlap between training and evaluation pairs even though only training-positive
records are explicitly added to the graph. The unavailable input records prevent measurement of the
resulting overlap. We therefore treat the historical model comparisons as exploratory and do not claim
evaluation on a verified pair-disjoint test set.

2.5
Literature-retrieval screen
The historical screen used language-model-assisted literature retrieval to look for publications supporting
sampled chemical–disease pairs. The expanded screen examined seven windows of 1,000 ranked records,
beginning at ranks 1, 10,001, 20,001, 30,001, 40,001, 50,001 and 60,001. The first 1,000-record window
was also summarized as ten consecutive 100-record bins. An additional 1,000 pairs were sampled randomly
from vocabularies containing 16,171 chemical names and 6,193 disease names. The reported procedure
used the same retrieval approach for the ranked and random samples.

2.7
Evidence-linked interface specification
The existing interface design accepts a chemical name or CAS Registry Number, displays matched
chemical–disease relations as a graph, shows nonempty record attributes and exposes associated literature.
It distinguishes observed and predicted edges visually. This is an interface specification and design artifact;
system availability, user performance and end-to-end operation were not evaluated in this study.
Figure 1 places the modeling and evidence components in one workflow. The proposed extension
broadens the searchable entity class from environmental pollutants to chemicals, preserves gene nodes
and their relations, and links an evidence item to its source through PubMed, DOI and resolved Acemap
records [10]. Acemap identifiers would need an explicit mapping to PMID or DOI before article-level links
could be treated as reliable. These integrations define the intended system scope and are not presented as
completed experimental results.

Results
3.1
The exposure-data pilot does not establish a graph-model advantage
The reported exposure-data pilot uses a graph with 639 chemical nodes and 94 disease nodes. Table 2 gives
the original comparison. The MLP has the highest reported accuracy, recall, F1 and AUROC. Its AUROC
exceeds that of GAT by 0.0010 and that of GCN by 0.0138. Without repeated runs or paired prediction
records, the small difference between MLP and GAT cannot be interpreted as statistically resolved. The
result supports retaining a non-graph baseline in future experiments.

3.5
Case references illustrate why relation typing is necessary
The inspected publications support different interpretations (Table 4). Sulfamethoxazole and acute gen-
eralized exanthematous pustulosis appear in a case report with a positive lymphocyte transformation test
[11]. The lithium example concerns hyperosmolar nonketotic coma with a hypothesized indirect pathway
through suspected lithium-associated nephrogenic diabetes insipidus [12]. The octreotide paper reports
treatment of pancreatic pseudocysts [13]. Thus, finding literature for a chemical–disease pair can recover
an adverse reaction, an indirect clinical pathway or a therapeutic effect.

4
Discussion
4.1
Implications for association discovery
The reported literature yields provide a reason to investigate score-guided review further, but the most
defensible current outcome is the organization of potentially relevant literature leads. The class-ambiguous
export prevents interpretation of its leading records as novel positive predictions. Even after correcting the
export, literature-based assessment would need to distinguish rediscovery from evidence published after the
graph was frozen. Publications already underlying CTD cannot serve simultaneously as training evidence
and independent confirmation of a prediction derived from that evidence.
The exposure-data comparison also places a useful constraint on model claims. The MLP is numerically
competitive with or better than the two reported graph models. This may reflect the dataset, split, sampling
scheme or the usefulness of learned entity identities; the current evidence cannot separate those explanations.
A graph model should be justified by evaluation on matched splits and appropriate simple baselines, not by
the presence of graph-structured data alone.
Gene overlap provides a second caution. Conditioning on mappable records changes the reported rate
from 12.8% to 20.0%, but it does not improve the comparison against a null that uses all 1,000 records.
The observed rate remains below the recorded permutation mean. This result argues for investigating
mapping quality, chemical and disease coverage, and gene-degree effects before treating shared paths
as corroboration. A gene can participate in many recorded relationships without mediating the specific
chemical–disease association being reviewed.
4.2
A broader chemical resource with traceable evidence
The system should represent “chemical” as the general entity class and retain pollutant, pharmaceutical,
endogenous compound, mixture and other categories as explicit annotations where justified. A CAS number
or a database name should not silently collapse a mixture, a chemical class and a single molecular structure.
Source identifiers and mapping decisions should be preserved alongside display names. Extending the
resource in this way increases scope while keeping environmental exposure questions distinguishable from
drug-repurposing questions.
Closer Acemap integration is most useful at the evidence item rather than the search-page level. A
candidate relationship should expose its PMID, DOI, resolved Acemap article record and relevant source

titled paper. The planned evidence record would also carry the quoted supporting passage, relation type,
study design, organism, dose or concentration, timing and adjudication status. These fields would allow
users to assess whether the link supports the specific claim under consideration.
Gene nodes should be added with typed relations such as chemical–gene interaction and gene–disease
association. CTD already supports integrated mechanistic exploration, including connections among
chemicals, genes, phenotypes and diseases [14]; the intended contribution here is their traceable use within
this workflow. Edge direction, biological effect and evidence source need to accompany the topology.
For prediction, a heterogeneous graph may integrate these relations; for explanation, the interface should
display the actual supporting path and its publications. An explanation path need not be a feature used by
the predictor, and the interface should state whether it was part of the training graph, added after prediction
or obtained from an independent source. The present overlap screen is a starting point for such a view, not
a validated mechanism model.
4.3
Evaluation needed to distinguish retrieval, prediction and validation
A confirmatory evaluation should begin with an immutable, dated graph snapshot and unique pair-level
splits. All occurrences of a held-out pair must be excluded from training labels and message-passing
edges. In a heterogeneous model, derived edges that encode a held-out target require separate leakage
checks. Random transductive evaluation should be complemented by chemical-disjoint, disease-disjoint
and temporal tests. The ID-embedding baseline cannot by itself establish performance for unseen entities;
that setting requires explicit handling of new nodes and their features.
The comparator set should include degree-based scoring, an embedding MLP and matched graph models,
with the same data, sampling, tuning budget and candidate universe. Average precision, precision at a fixed
review budget and calibration are useful alongside AUROC because real candidate spaces are not balanced
collections of known positives and sampled unknowns. Repeated seeds and paired uncertainty estimates
are needed before claiming model differences.
Literature assessment should predefine the query procedure and evidence categories, conceal model
scores from reviewers where feasible, and retain pair-level judgments. A time cutoff should separate prior
literature from genuinely subsequent evidence. Random and degree-matched controls address different
questions and should both be named accurately. For gene context, mapping coverage and path rate should
be reported together, and null comparisons should account for gene-degree and source overlap. These
procedures are a prospective validation design, not experiments completed in the present study; a concrete
specification is provided in Supplementary Information, Section S6.
4.4
Limitations
The study relies partly on aggregate experimental summaries. Missing source snapshots, split manifests
and complete labeled predictions prevent reproduction of the historical model metrics and literature yields.
The retained training sources contain incompatible entry points and do not constitute a verified execution
environment for every reported baseline. No repeated-run variability, deployment benchmark, controlled
user study or independent biological experiment is available.
The CTD-based binary task collapses evidence with different meanings, and the negative class comprises
unobserved pairs rather than established non-associations. Record-level splitting and repeated samples
may make evaluation optimistic. Literature retrieval can favor well-studied entities, can return therapeutic
evidence for an adverse-association question, and can reuse the database’s own supporting publications.
Name-based gene mapping can miss valid identifiers or merge incompatible names; gene-set intersection
discards dose, direction and context. These limitations constrain the results to exploratory association
review.

Conclusions
The existing chemical–disease work supports an exploratory workflow combining graph-based scoring with
accessible literature and gene-context views. The reported leading-window literature yield is higher than
that of random name pairs, while the available gene screen shows no enrichment relative to its recorded
permutation mean. Prediction-export semantics and relation typing are decisive for interpretation. Broader
chemical coverage, resolved Acemap links and explicit gene relations provide a coherent direction for
system development, with claims of discovery requiring reproducible positive predictions and independent
